## Inspiring change to Korea's rigid top-down hierarchical org culture
- Korea's hierarchical "pecking order" is rigid, and subordinates do not feel comfortable voicing their opinions and are generally expected to agree to and follow decisions that come from those in senior positions
- Nidhi spent a year constantly complaining about the org culture, and finally acted for a change by asking each member of the team to come to meetings with a brief presentation on how he/her viewed an issue/problem and how to address it. Thereafter, there was a more open discussion of ideas and communication, which stands in stark contrast to the status quo (subordinates generally keeping silent, and serving as yes men)

## Lowering the stakes and running a test case 
- Tyler observed the inefficiencies of managers(agents) in the Korean entertainment industry handling casting inquiries and other work relating to talent via Kakaotalk(i.e. Korea's Whatsapp). He noted that working off of chats resulted in misjudgments, wrong decisions, and omissions
- To his agent at the time, Tyler suggested setting up a Google Form and asking everyone interested in hiring specific talent to fill out the form. His agent's initial pushback was somewhat typical of Koreans: he worried that the number of inquiries would drop, the people looking to hire talent would think that they were overstepping boundaries, and they would end up getting a bad rap in the industry because such a method is just culturally "not how it's done here"
- Upon hearing this, most people would give up and drop the idea. But Tyler countered with a proposal: test it out for a week or two and see the results
- Within two weeks of trying the new method, the response was overwhelmingly positive. Agents(managers) are already too busy handling their everyday tasks. The benefit of this method is uniformity, clarity, reduction in errors, and record-keeping. A standard form is sent out to all prospectors, and thus each inquiry is easier to manage with the same form. Because everything is written down, there are fewer ambiguities, and inquirers get an auto-generated response giving them a written record as well.
- This was the basis upon which Tyler began his own entertainment agency

## Disregard advice from well-meaning, but inexperienced people 
- People like to share what they know, what they've heard about so-and-so business or venture. But do a double take: has this person actually done that which you are now trying to do? In other words, do they have skin in the game? If not, don't listen to them
- And even if they DO have experience, take caution: what worked yesterday may not work today. And the way that person did it was his or her way. Even if you follow his or her style to the letter, it might not work out for you. You just have to take away what you can and then apply it your way.


## Don't ask for permission
- Tyler noted that in Korea, people seek permission to do something. They seek some sort of approval when setting out on some venture. Be it friends, family, teachers, etc.
- Interestingly, he noted that in Korea, the street signs show at which intersections you may do a U-turn. I thought about this and realized that in the US and Canada, by default, you can do a U-turn at all intersections on a green light. There are signs for intersections where you may not.
- This made me reflect on prescribing a normative approach versus  Tim Ferriss' approach of "don't ask permission, ask forgiveness" and the merits of both. 
- Tyler also noted that nobody should or can ask permission, and neither is it correct to ask for permission. Who could give that kind of permission? It's your life at the end of the day. I would venture to add that should one go about acquiring this permission from an elder, a friend, a parent, etc., will this person granting the permission take responsibility for your decision in the event things go awry? Think about that.

## Failure isn't about your lack of talent or skills
- it goes without saying that you need to have a certain base level of skills to be successful
- but beyond that,  you shouldn't beat yourself over the head for a failure, blaming it on your lack of talent or skills
- Rather, it may be that there was a mistake or error in the approach, and more often than not, you were just plain unlucky, the timing wasn't right, market conditions weren't right, the competitor outsmarted you, etc.

## Don't make decisions from a position of fear
- Tyler argues that well-meaning people often speak to us purportedly out of empathy, but that actually is a form of gaslighting
- Koreans will say things like "I think you may be unaware but..." "I'm telling you this because I'm on your side. Everyone else's thinking the same thing, but I'm letting you know because I like you / care for you" "You say you don't mind failing, but that's because you've never failed before. You know how hard it is?" "Have you ever really failed hard, hit rock bottom?" All these things make you freeze up and get held up by fear
- Nidhi apparently heard someone tell her "You'd be prettier if you shed 5 kgs"
- Tyler advises to not make rash decisions out of fear and gaslighting
